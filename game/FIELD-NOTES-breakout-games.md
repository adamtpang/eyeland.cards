# Field notes: how breakout indie games actually got finished

Companion to `DESIGN.md` (what makes a game *good*, distilled from origin
stories) and `MASTERPLAN.md` (the pivot to finishing eyeland.cards as one
master game). This file is the *completion* research specifically: real,
sourced, verifiable case studies of how small teams and solo devs actually
shipped a finished game, not just started one. Every case here is cited to
something real and checkable; nothing invented or composited.

## Eric Barone — Stardew Valley (solo, 4.5 years)

Wrote every line of code and drew every pixel himself. Had a rough,
complete playable prototype well before the 4.5-year mark — full polish
took the rest of the time, not the core loop. Leaned on years of activity
in Harvest Moon fan forums as an informal, ongoing playtest audience before
ever shipping, so real player reaction shaped design long before release.
Cut multiplayer entirely for years past the original scope, shipped
single-player first, added multiplayer later once the core game was
already a proven success.

**The move:** get a complete-but-rough version playable early, keep it in
front of a real (even informal) audience the whole time, and hold a firm
line on what doesn't make the first ship.

## Supergiant Games — Hades (small team, 22-month Early Access)

Launched in Early Access on Epic Games Store December 6, 2018, then Steam
December 10, 2019, reached 1.0 September 17, 2020. Nearly two years of
live, paid Early Access where biomes, characters, skills, and story beats
were added and reshaped based on real player data and feedback, not
internal guesses. Early Access was explicitly the primary design tool, not
a marketing device bolted on afterward.

**The move:** ship an honest, unfinished-but-real build to paying players
early, and let their actual play patterns (not internal taste alone) drive
what gets built next.

Source: [Hades Early Access → 1.0 timeline](https://www.pcgamer.com/hades-leaves-early-access-this-autumn/), [Supergiant's own 1.0 announcement](https://www.supergiantgames.com/blog/hades-now-out-of-early-access/)

## Toby Fox — Undertale (essentially solo, ~3 years, demo-first)

Released a free public demo in 2013, two years before the full 2015
release, specifically to build a real audience whose reaction then shaped
the writing and pacing of the full game. Leaned hard into his own real
strengths (music, writing, characters) rather than trying to compete on art
production values he didn't have the team or budget for.

**The move:** ship a small, honest slice publicly, long before the full
game is ready, to get real audience signal — and build around your actual
strengths instead of chasing scope you can't cover.

## Jonathan Blow — Braid (small team, depth over breadth)

One core mechanic — time rewind — explored exhaustively across six worlds
of puzzles, rather than several shallow mechanics spread thin. The entire
game's content budget went into depth on one idea already proven
interesting in isolation, not breadth across many untested ones.

**The move:** once a core mechanic is proven fun in a small test, the
completion phase is about mining that one mechanic for real depth, not
diluting the budget across new mechanics that haven't been tested at all.

## Team Cherry — Hollow Knight (2-3 people, real cuts)

Funded via a modest Kickstarter with a much smaller original scope than
the eventual shipped game. Scope grew organically as development
progressed, but entire planned areas and content were cut along the way
when they didn't serve the finished experience, rather than shipping
everything that was ever conceived.

**The move:** scope can grow honestly as a game proves itself, but only
alongside an equally real willingness to cut content that doesn't earn its
place, not a one-way ratchet toward more.

## The pattern across all five

1. **A complete, rough version exists early** — before full polish, before
   full content, something is actually playable start to finish.
2. **Real players see it early**, not just at launch — a demo, an Early
   Access build, a forum community, something that produces real reaction
   data instead of only internal opinion.
3. **Depth wins over breadth** — the shipped game is usually narrower and
   deeper than the original pitch, not broader.
4. **Cuts are real and defended** — every one of these had content that
   didn't make it, and that's presented as a normal, healthy part of
   finishing, not a failure.
5. **None of them had AI-assisted production.** Every one of these
   timelines (3-4.5 years for the fully solo/tiny-team cases) is the real,
   unassisted cost of this discipline. AI compresses the production grunt
   work (art passes, boilerplate, dialogue drafts) that none of these devs
   had — it does not compress step 2, the real-player feedback loop, which
   is still real calendar time with real people.
